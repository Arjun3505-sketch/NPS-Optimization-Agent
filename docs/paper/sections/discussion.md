# 5. Discussion

**Why no tier effect?** A glide path acts on returns; income volatility acts on contributions. Over a window
with a +2.9% equity premium, extra equity improves every cohort's odds of reaching its target, and the
shortfall metric (ratio to cumulative contributions) is dominated by market returns. Withdrawals reduce
*levels* sharply but, because the optimal path is chosen by ranking candidates, they do not change the ranking.
The break-even analysis makes the mechanism explicit: the allocation flips with the premium, identically for all
tiers.

**What would change the conclusion.** (i) Objectives that make the *target itself* volatility-dependent
(e.g. income-replacement goals scaled to a cohort's earnings); (ii) behavioral responses (selling after
losses); (iii) richer policy classes, including contribution-aware (state-dependent) rules rather than
age-only schedules - a policy that conditions on realized contribution history is the natural next test;
(iv) other return regimes with deep, long drawdowns.

**Policy implication.** Reforms that raise contribution regularity (auto-debit, smoothing buffers,
matched contributions) or add liquidity without forcing sales may matter more for informal workers than
re-tuning the equity glide path.

**On the negative result.** We report a chain of failed differentiation attempts, including one retracted
apparent positive (Section 4.2), because the negative result is robust to the assumptions we could perturb
and informative about where not to look.
